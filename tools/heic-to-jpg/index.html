Why HEIC files cause trouble

Since iOS 11, every iPhone and iPad saves photos in the HEIC format (High Efficiency Image Container) unless you change the setting. It is a clever format — the same photo takes about half the space of a JPG — but support outside Apple devices is still poor: Windows cannot open HEIC without extra codecs, Android phones and most websites and application-form portals reject it, and many printers and office apps have never heard of it. Converting to JPG solves it in seconds, because JPG is the one image format that opens literally everywhere — and this converter keeps your photo at its exact original dimensions, so nothing is cropped or resized behind your back.

How to use

  1. Add your HEIC photos. Drag & drop them, tap to select, or paste — up to 20 at a time. The first HEIC file loads a HEIC decoder in your browser (about 2 MB, once); after that, conversions are instant. (Other images, such as PNG, convert too.)
  2. Choose the output. JPG is the default. For JPG, set the quality (92% recommended) and pick white or black as the background for any transparent pixels — JPG cannot store transparency, so those pixels are flattened onto that colour.
  3. Convert and download. Every file shows its size before and after. Download each converted file on its own, or use Download all to save the whole batch one file after another.

JPG or PNG — which should you choose?

  • An iPhone photo for a form, portal or document: JPG. Universally accepted, small file, and the 92% quality default keeps it looking identical.
  • A photo you want to keep pixel-perfect: PNG. No quality is lost at all — the file will be larger.
  • A photo a website rejected as “unsupported file type”: JPG, white background, default quality — the safest combination there is.

Frequently asked questions

What is a HEIC file?

HEIC is the photo format iPhones and iPads have saved in by default since iOS 11 (2017). It keeps photos at roughly half the file size of a JPG at the same quality — but Windows, Android and most websites and upload forms still cannot open it, which is why iPhone photos often seem stuck on the phone.

Why do I need to convert HEIC to JPG at all?

Because JPG is accepted practically everywhere and HEIC is not. If a form portal, a website, an office app or a Windows PC refuses your iPhone photo or calls it an unsupported file type, converting it to JPG is the fix — the photo itself keeps its exact size.

How can this page open HEIC files if browsers cannot?

Browsers cannot decode HEIC on their own, so the first time you add a HEIC file this page loads a HEIC decoder (about 2 MB, downloaded once) and runs it inside your browser. The conversion still happens entirely on your device — your photos are never uploaded to any server.

Will the image dimensions or quality change?

The pixel dimensions never change — a 4032×3024 iPhone photo becomes a 4032×3024 JPG. JPG is a lossy format, so a little quality is traded for a much smaller file; at the default 92% quality setting the difference is not visible in normal use. If you need a perfectly lossless copy, choose PNG instead.

Are my photos uploaded to your server?

No. The conversion happens inside your browser on your own device — the converter, and the HEIC decoder it loads, run in JavaScript on your phone or computer, and your photos are never sent anywhere, never stored and never seen by anyone.

How many photos can I convert at once?

Up to 20 photos in one batch. Each converted file can be downloaded individually, and the Download all button saves every converted file one after another (your browser may ask you to allow multiple downloads).

Can this tool convert other formats too?

Yes — the page is made for HEIC and HEIF, but any image your browser can open (PNG, JPG, GIF, WebP, AVIF) can be dropped in and converted to JPG or PNG the same way.
