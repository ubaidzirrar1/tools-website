Why forms reject your photo — and it's rarely your fault

Every application portal checks your uploads with software, and software is pedantic. It typically verifies three separate things: that the file is a JPG, that its size in kilobytes sits inside a stated band, and that its pixel dimensions match the specification exactly — 200 × 230 means 200 × 230, not “about that size”. A photo can pass two checks and fail the third, and many portals respond with nothing more helpful than “upload failed”. Candidates then shrink the file again and again, making it blurrier without ever touching the actual problem, which was the dimensions all along.

The KB band surprises people in the other direction too. Because a band has a minimum as well as a maximum, a very clean, small photo can be rejected for being too small — under 20 KB for an SSC photo, under 10 KB for a signature. Making a file bigger without touching the picture sounds impossible, but it is a standard, legitimate technique, explained below.

A photo and a signature are different jobs

Forms ask for both because they do different work. The photograph identifies you — invigilators compare it with your face and with the photo on your admit card — so it needs a clear, recent, front-facing image with your whole face inside the frame. The signature verifies you: it is compared against the signature you make on the attendance sheet on exam day. That is why the two files have such different shapes: a tall 200 × 230 portrait frame for the photo, and a wide, short 140 × 60 strip for the signature. This tool keeps a separate workspace for each, so you can prepare both, one after the other, without starting again.

How to photograph your signature so it passes first time

Most rejected signatures fail on the photograph, not the handwriting. Sign on a plain white sheet — never on ruled or squared paper, because the lines get baked into the image. Use a dark blue or black pen and sign slightly larger and bolder than you would on a cheque; thin, faint strokes can disappear when the image is reduced to 140 × 60 pixels. Rest the paper flat, photograph it from directly above in bright, even light, and fill the frame with the signature area so there is less background to crop away. Watch for the classic fault: your own shadow falling across the paper, which turns half the “white” background grey. If the signature looks clear and dark on your phone screen at arm's length, it will survive the resize comfortably.

How this tool works

When you press the Make button, the tool draws exactly the region inside your crop frame onto a fresh canvas at precisely the preset's pixel size — 200 × 230 means a canvas of exactly 200 by 230 pixels — against a white background, the standard forms expect. It then encodes that canvas as a JPEG, searching for the highest quality setting that still fits under the maximum KB. If a minimum applies and the picture at top quality is still below it, the tool appends empty JPEG comment segments — a legal part of the JPEG format that viewers and decoders simply skip — until the file size enters the band. Nothing about the picture changes; the file just carries a little invisible ballast. Finally, the result panel states the exact dimensions, the exact size, and a plain-language confirmation that you are inside the band, so you can upload with confidence.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between this tool and Compress Image to KB?

That tool changes only the file size (KB) and keeps your photo's original proportions. This tool changes size AND dimensions together: the output is exactly the pixel width and height the form demands (for example 200 × 230 px) AND inside the required KB band. If your form lists pixels, you need this tool; if it lists only a KB limit, either tool works.

My photo was already the right KB, but the form still rejected it. Why?

Because most forms check three things, not one: the file format (JPG), the file size in KB, and the exact pixel dimensions. A 40 KB photo that is 800 × 1000 pixels still fails an SSC upload that demands 200 × 230 pixels. This tool sets all three at once, which is why the result panel shows the exact dimensions next to the size.

Why would a form reject a file for being too SMALL?

Several exam portals — SSC and IBPS among them — specify a band such as 20–50 KB, and their software rejects files below the minimum just as firmly as files above the maximum. If your cropped photo encodes to less than the minimum at top quality, this tool adds a small amount of harmless padding data inside the JPEG file (in comment segments decoders ignore) to bring it into the band. The picture itself is not changed at all.

How should I photograph my signature?

Sign with a dark blue or black pen on a plain white sheet of paper — no ruled lines — and sign a little larger than usual so the strokes are bold. Photograph it straight-on in bright, even light, filling the frame with the signature area, and check there are no shadows across the paper. Then use the Signature tab here: crop tightly around the signature with the locked 140 × 60 frame.

Is my photo or signature uploaded to your server?

No. Everything — cropping, resizing, encoding — runs inside your browser on your own device. Your files are never sent anywhere, never stored, and never seen by anyone. The tool even keeps working if you switch off your internet after the page has loaded.
